# DANMACHI: RETELLING OF THE LEGEND

# Chapter Seventy-Two — Purification

*Some things could be cleansed. Others could only be contained. And there were things in this world that should never have been created in the first place.*

---

### I. The Difference Between Saving and Purifying

The morning arrived quietly.

Sunlight poured over Orario's enormous city walls, spilling across the rooftops and illuminating the countless streets that gradually came alive beneath it. Merchants opened their establishments, adventurers gathered outside taverns, and the familiar commotion of the city resumed as though nothing particularly unusual had happened during the previous night.

From a distance, everything appeared peaceful.

But peace was often an illusion created by the absence of visible conflict.

Within the Hearth Manor, a considerably more complicated problem occupied the attention of its inhabitants.

Bell Cranel stood beside a table covered with scattered documents, several diagrams, and a collection of notes that had gradually expanded beyond what he initially intended.

Across from him sat Ein.

She was unusually quiet.

Her hands rested on her lap, and her eyes followed Bell as he examined another page. Her expression was difficult to interpret, although the occasional movement of her fingers suggested that she was considerably more nervous than she wanted to appear.

Bell eventually placed the document down.

"How are you feeling?"

Ein blinked.

"That is the third time you've asked me."

"Because you haven't given me a particularly convincing answer."

"I'm fine."

"That's the fourth time you've used that answer."

Ein lowered her gaze.

Bell smiled faintly.

He pulled a chair closer and sat opposite her.

"Listen. I don't expect you to understand everything immediately. But I need you to tell me when something feels wrong. Anything unusual. Changes in your senses, your thoughts, your emotions, even something that might seem insignificant."

"Why?"

"Because we're trying to understand what's happening to you."

Ein looked at her hands.

There was a moment of silence.

Then she asked something that made Bell's expression soften.

"What if I don't like what we discover?"

Bell considered his answer.

"Then we'll deal with it."

"What if the answer is that I cannot return to normal?"

He did not immediately respond.

It would have been easy to reassure her.

He could have promised that everything would work out, that there would eventually be a solution, and that whatever had happened to her could be reversed.

But those promises would have been meaningless.

Bell had encountered enough strange phenomena to understand the danger of assuming that every problem possessed a convenient solution.

Instead, he leaned forward.

"Then we'll find out what normal means for you."

Ein looked at him.

"That's not the same thing."

"No. It isn't."

He smiled.

"But I would rather understand what we're dealing with than force you into something that might hurt you."

Her expression changed slightly.

For a moment, she seemed unable to find the appropriate words.

Bell returned his attention to the documents.

There were several possibilities worth investigating.

The transformation.

The relationship between the two Filvis identities.

The nature of the corruption.

And, perhaps most importantly, whether the condition affecting Ein was an irreversible transformation or a state maintained by something external.

He had no intention of assuming that those possibilities were interchangeable.

A transformed body was one problem.

A transformed soul was another.

And a transformation affecting both was something considerably more complicated.

"Bell?"

"Hm?"

"Are you going to experiment on me?"

He stopped writing.

Then he looked at her.

"Not without your permission."

"Even if it helps?"

"Especially then."

Ein tilted her head.

Bell tapped the document with his pen.

"There's a difference between treating someone and using someone as an experimental subject. I don't intend to forget that distinction."

She studied him for several seconds.

Then, slowly, she smiled.

It was a small expression.

But it was genuine.

"Thank you."

Bell nodded.

"Now, there's something I want to try."

Her smile disappeared.

"...What?"

"An examination."

"An examination?"

"Nothing invasive. I want to observe how your condition responds to different forms of magical interference. If we can identify what sustains the transformation, we might discover a way to reverse it."

Ein hesitated.

"Will it hurt?"

"I don't know."

Her expression stiffened.

Bell immediately continued.

"Which is why we'll begin with the safest possible method. If anything feels uncomfortable, we stop."

He stood and began clearing a portion of the table.

A small collection of prepared materials emerged from a nearby container.

There were measuring instruments, a few crystals, and several sheets of paper covered in diagrams.

Bell had no intention of pretending that he possessed a complete understanding of the phenomenon.

He didn't.

But he had a starting point.

And sometimes, a sufficiently careful starting point was worth more than a hundred confident assumptions.

---

### II. The Shape of a Monster

The examination lasted considerably longer than Bell anticipated.

Not because anything particularly dangerous happened.

Quite the opposite.

The results were frustratingly inconclusive.

Bell observed several irregularities in the way Ein's condition interacted with the magical measurements. Certain readings appeared consistent, while others changed depending on the method used.

There was no obvious pattern.

At least, not one he could confidently identify.

He eventually put down his pen.

"Interesting."

Ein looked at him.

"Is that good?"

"Not necessarily."

"That's not reassuring."

Bell chuckled.

"Sorry."

He examined the notes again.

There was something peculiar about the results.

The transformation did not appear to behave like a simple physical alteration. Nor did the measurements support the assumption that everything could be explained through ordinary magical contamination.

He needed more information.

Preferably from someone who understood the underlying phenomenon better than he did.

"Bell?"

"Yes?"

"Can I ask something?"

"Of course."

"Do you think I'm a monster?"

The question was delivered quietly.

Bell stopped moving.

He looked at her for a moment.

"Why are you asking?"

"Because I know what happened to me."

Ein lowered her eyes.

"I remember what I became. I remember the things I could do. And sometimes..."

Her fingers tightened.

"Sometimes I wonder whether those things are still inside me."

Bell listened without interrupting.

Ein continued.

"I don't want to hurt anyone. I don't want to become something that other people have to fear. But I don't know whether wanting something is enough."

Bell leaned back.

He understood the question.

Not entirely, perhaps, but enough.

"Ein."

She looked up.

"Being dangerous and being evil aren't the same thing."

He paused.

"Neither is being different and being monstrous."

"But—"

"Listen first."

His voice remained gentle.

"If someone has the ability to destroy an entire building, that doesn't automatically make them a monster. It makes them someone who possesses an ability that requires responsibility."

He glanced at the documents.

"And if something happened to you that changed your body, your instincts, or even the way your magic works, that doesn't automatically erase everything you were before."

Ein remained silent.

Bell continued.

"I won't pretend that your condition doesn't matter. It does. We need to understand it, especially if there's a possibility that it could harm you or someone else."

He looked directly at her.

"But I refuse to make your entire existence revolve around it."

For several seconds, the room remained quiet.

Then Ein slowly nodded.

Bell returned to his notes.

He had another problem to consider.

If the transformation could be reversed, what exactly needed to be removed?

A foreign influence?

A magical structure?

A corrupted source of energy?

Or something much more fundamental?

He wrote a single word across the top of a fresh page.

**Purification.**

Not destruction.

Not suppression.

Purification.

The distinction mattered.

Destroying the source of a transformation might also destroy the person sustaining it. Suppressing it could merely delay the inevitable.

What he needed was a method of separating the harmful influence from whatever constituted Ein's original self.

And that required understanding the difference between the two.

Bell stared at the page.

For the first time that morning, he felt that he was approaching the correct question.

Not how to remove the monster.

But how to save the person without destroying what remained of her.

### III. A Different Kind of Betrayal

Far from the Hearth Manor, another Filvis was beginning to question the foundation of her existence.

The room was dim.

Heavy curtains concealed the windows, allowing only narrow strips of daylight to penetrate the interior. Several books occupied the shelves, while an assortment of documents had been arranged across a writing desk.

Filvis sat before them.

She had not slept particularly well.

Not that sleep had ever been especially comforting recently.

Her thoughts kept returning to the same question.

How much longer?

The sound of footsteps approached.

She immediately straightened.

The door opened.

Dionysus entered with his usual composed expression.

"Good morning, Filvis."

"Lord Dionysus."

He smiled.

"Have you made any progress?"

Filvis lowered her eyes toward the documents.

"Some."

"Excellent."

He approached the desk and examined the papers.

There were several observations regarding the creatures encountered in the deeper regions of the Dungeon, alongside notes concerning their unusual properties.

Dionysus studied them for a moment.

Then his expression changed.

"These are incomplete."

"I was still investigating."

"Then investigate faster."

Filvis remained silent.

Dionysus turned another page.

"The information obtained from Loki was disappointingly limited. Apparently, the captain has developed an interest in asking questions."

His smile remained.

But something beneath it had become noticeably colder.

"An unfortunate habit."

Filvis carefully observed him.

"Perhaps they simply don't understand the situation."

Dionysus looked at her.

"Perhaps."

He returned the documents to the table.

"Regardless, we cannot afford further delays."

His fingers moved across the surface.

"The other Filvis has become a considerable inconvenience."

Filvis did not react.

Inside, however, something tightened.

Dionysus continued.

"She has managed to escape the circumstances that should have kept her under control. Worse, she has attached herself to someone who appears to possess an unreasonable talent for interfering with matters he doesn't understand."

Bell Cranel.

The name remained unspoken.

It didn't need to be.

Filvis knew precisely whom he meant.

"What do you intend to do?"

Dionysus smiled.

"Nothing particularly complicated."

He approached the window.

"People are remarkably predictable when they believe they are protecting something precious."

Filvis watched his reflection.

"They become careless. Emotional. They abandon perfectly reasonable precautions because they cannot bear the thought of losing what they have acquired."

He turned.

"And that is where we shall begin."

Filvis lowered her head.

"I understand."

"Good."

Dionysus approached the door.

"Prepare the necessary arrangements. I want the next stage ready before our little problem becomes any more troublesome."

The door closed.

Silence returned.

Filvis remained seated.

For several seconds, she did not move.

Then she slowly exhaled.

The expression on her face changed.

It was not relief.

Nor was it fear.

It was something considerably more complicated.

She looked toward the documents Dionysus had left behind.

There were several names.

Several locations.

And a collection of arrangements that, taken individually, appeared almost ordinary.

But she knew what they meant.

Dionysus was preparing to manipulate the situation.

And this time, she had no intention of allowing him to control every part of it.

She reached beneath the desk and retrieved a small folded paper.

It contained a short list of instructions.

Nothing elaborate.

Nothing that would immediately attract attention.

But it was enough to begin.

She had already made her decision.

If Dionysus wanted to use her as a piece in his game, then she would simply have to become a piece that moved in the wrong direction.

A quiet betrayal.

A carefully arranged inconvenience.

And, if necessary, something considerably more dangerous.

Filvis folded the paper.

Her expression became cold.

"I'm sorry, Lord Dionysus."

She placed it inside her clothing.

"But I won't help you do this."

### IV. The Inheritance of Blood

Bell had always considered knowledge a dangerous thing.

Not because knowledge itself was inherently harmful.

Rather, because people rarely agreed on what should be done with it.

Some sought knowledge to understand the world.

Others sought it to improve their circumstances.

And there were those who sought it because they wanted the power to decide how the world ought to function.

The latter category was particularly troublesome.

That afternoon, Bell found himself examining a collection of documents that had little to do with the immediate problem of Ein's purification.

At least, not directly.

The subject was hereditary traits.

More specifically, the deliberate cultivation of desirable characteristics through controlled reproduction.

Eugenics.

He had encountered enough references to understand the general idea, although the information available to him was fragmented.

The concept itself wasn't particularly complicated.

Identify desirable characteristics.

Determine how they were inherited.

Encourage their continuation.

Discourage characteristics considered undesirable.

Repeat the process across generations.

On paper, it could be presented as a rational method of improving a population.

In practice, however, the moment someone began deciding which lives were worth preserving, the entire concept became considerably more dangerous.

Bell turned another page.

There were references to inherited magical aptitude, physical characteristics, compatibility, and the potential advantages of particular bloodlines.

Some of the observations were legitimate subjects of biological investigation.

Others were considerably more speculative.

Bell frowned.

There was a difference between studying inheritance and attempting to engineer an entire population.

The former could improve medicine, help people understand their bodies, and provide explanations for certain inherited conditions.

The latter could become an instrument of political control.

He leaned back.

*And that's where the problem begins.*

He considered the implications.

Suppose someone discovered that a particular lineage possessed an unusually high affinity for magic.

Would that information remain a scientific curiosity?

Or would powerful Familias begin competing to acquire members of that lineage?

What if someone discovered a hereditary predisposition toward particular abilities?

Would families begin arranging marriages to preserve those traits?

And what would happen to children who failed to meet the expectations placed upon them?

Bell's expression darkened.

There were already enough examples of people being valued according to their usefulness.

Adventurers were measured by their Levels.

Mages were judged by their magical capabilities.

Familias competed for resources, influence, and exceptional individuals.

Now imagine extending that mentality beyond an individual's achievements.

Imagine judging someone before they were even born.

He placed the document down.

*That's not improvement.*

*That's ownership.*

The realization was uncomfortable.

Not because the subject was unfamiliar, but because the underlying logic was disturbingly recognizable.

A person could be reduced to a collection of traits.

A collection of traits could become a measurement of usefulness.

And usefulness could become the justification for deciding who deserved opportunities, protection, or even the right to make their own choices.

Bell rubbed his forehead.

There was another complication.

The DanMachi world possessed phenomena that made the subject considerably more dangerous than ordinary hereditary selection.

Falna.

Skills.

Magic.

Excelia.

The influence of divine blessings upon mortal development.

The relationship between inherited aptitude and acquired growth was already complicated enough without introducing deliberate attempts to manipulate it.

A person's potential did not necessarily determine their eventual accomplishments.

Nor did a person's inherited characteristics guarantee that they would develop in a particular direction.

Experience mattered.

Choices mattered.

Circumstances mattered.

And sometimes, the person who appeared least promising became someone extraordinary.

Bell glanced toward the window.

*If someone starts treating potential as destiny, they're going to make a terrible mistake.*

He returned to the documents.

There was something else he wanted to investigate.

The possibility that certain individuals were being selected, cultivated, or manipulated because of their compatibility with particular magical phenomena.

He did not yet possess evidence that such a program existed.

He wasn't going to pretend otherwise.

But the possibility was worth considering.

Especially given the circumstances surrounding Ein and the other Filvis.

He wrote several questions.

- Is the transformation hereditary, artificial, or externally imposed?
- Does magical compatibility determine susceptibility?
- Can an individual's condition be altered without destroying their original identity?
- Are multiple individuals being subjected to similar procedures?
- Who benefits from the existence of such individuals?

The final question received considerably more attention than the others.

Bell stared at it.

*Who benefits?*

It was a simple question.

But it often revealed more than asking who was responsible.

People could conceal their involvement.

They could destroy documents, manipulate witnesses, and manufacture explanations.

But identifying the beneficiaries of a particular arrangement could expose the structure behind it.

He closed the notebook.

For now, he had theories.

Nothing more.

But he intended to keep investigating.

### V. The God Who Was Running Out of Time

Dionysus hated the silence.

It had become increasingly difficult to enjoy the things that once brought him pleasure.

Wine tasted dull.

Music felt repetitive.

Even the company of others had begun to irritate him.

It was an unfortunate development.

After all, he had spent considerable time cultivating an environment where everything proceeded according to his expectations.

And now?

Everything was becoming increasingly difficult to control.

He stood before a large mirror.

His reflection stared back at him.

For several seconds, he simply examined it.

Then he laughed.

A short, humorless sound.

"How amusing."

He raised a hand toward the mirror.

The image remained unchanged.

He lowered it.

The problem was not merely that his plans were encountering resistance.

That was expected.

Plans could be disrupted.

People could be unpredictable.

Even the most carefully arranged circumstances occasionally produced unexpected results.

But the rate at which he was losing influence had become unacceptable.

His Familia had suffered losses.

His investigation into the Dungeon had attracted unwanted attention.

The information he needed was becoming increasingly difficult to obtain.

And the situation involving Filvis had developed into something he had not anticipated.

The other Filvis was no longer where she belonged.

Worse, the one standing before him was beginning to develop ideas of her own.

Dionysus closed his eyes.

*How troublesome.*

There was a knock.

"Enter."

The door opened.

A subordinate stepped inside.

"My lord."

Dionysus turned.

"What is it?"

The subordinate placed an envelope on the table.

Dionysus stared at it.

He recognized the seal.

His expression became noticeably less pleasant.

"Already?"

"Yes, my lord."

He dismissed the subordinate with a gesture.

Once alone, Dionysus opened the envelope.

The contents were brief.

Far too brief.

The message was not a request for information.

It was a reminder.

His recent activities had attracted attention from the Council of the Dark Factions.

They were aware of the deaths.

They were aware of the failures.

And, more importantly, they were aware that his influence had begun to diminish.

Dionysus slowly lowered the paper.

For a moment, he did not move.

Then he laughed.

This time, there was genuine bitterness in it.

"So they've noticed."

He placed the letter on the table.

The Council was not an organization that tolerated prolonged incompetence.

Its members had their own interests, their own resources, and their own ambitions.

They did not necessarily care about his personal difficulties.

They cared about results.

And he was no longer producing enough of them.

He walked toward the window.

The city stretched beneath him.

Beautiful.

Busy.

Oblivious.

He had once believed that the circumstances surrounding his plans were sufficiently favorable to ensure success.

Now he found himself calculating how much time remained before the situation became irreversible.

He had lost too much.

Too many pieces.

Too much influence.

Too much freedom to operate without interference.

And the worst part?

He couldn't simply abandon everything.

There was too much at stake.

He needed a victory.

A meaningful one.

Something substantial enough to remind the Council that he was still useful.

His fingers tightened around the edge of the windowsill.

"Bell Cranel..."

The name escaped his lips.

A small smile returned.

Perhaps the young adventurer had become more troublesome than expected.

Perhaps he was even more useful than Dionysus had initially realized.

Either way, the situation demanded a change.

He could no longer afford to play the same game.

He needed someone else to make the next move.

Someone whose involvement would not immediately expose him.

Someone who understood the value of patience.

And, more importantly, someone who had considerably fewer reservations about getting their hands dirty.

Dionysus returned to the table.

He opened another drawer.

Inside were several documents bearing markings that few people in Orario would recognize.

He examined them.

Then he selected one.

His smile widened.

"Perhaps it's time."

### VI. The Council's Shadow

The meeting took place somewhere that did not appear on any ordinary map.

It was a chamber concealed beneath an old building, separated from the surface by several layers of stone and a series of passages that had been deliberately designed to discourage unwanted visitors.

The architecture was old.

Older than the building above it.

Possibly older than several of the organizations that had once occupied the surrounding district.

Its walls were constructed from dark stone, and the ceiling disappeared into an almost complete darkness.

A long table occupied the center.

Several figures sat around it.

Some wore elaborate clothing.

Others preferred simple garments.

A few kept their faces concealed.

None appeared particularly interested in conversation.

They were not gathered to exchange pleasantries.

They were gathered because one of their members had become a problem.

A voice eventually broke the silence.

"Dionysus."

The god's name carried no warmth.

"He has become unreliable."

Another figure shifted slightly.

"His resources have diminished."

"His Familia has suffered losses."

"And his recent investigation has attracted attention from the Guild and the Loki Familia."

A pause followed.

Then another voice spoke.

"He was supposed to provide results."

"He still might."

"How long are we expected to wait?"

No one answered immediately.

The chamber remained silent.

Finally, one of the figures leaned forward.

"His situation is not our only concern."

Several heads turned.

"The matter involving the Filvis identities has become increasingly complicated."

Another figure responded.

"Is the information confirmed?"

"No."

"Then why are we discussing it?"

"Because the possibility alone is sufficient to warrant attention."

A document was placed on the table.

It contained several observations.

Nothing conclusive.

Nothing that would establish the complete circumstances.

But enough to suggest that the situation was connected to something considerably larger than Dionysus's personal ambitions.

One of the figures examined it.

Then quietly asked:

"Who else knows?"

"That remains uncertain."

A second document followed.

This one contained a different marking.

A black hand.

The room became noticeably quieter.

Someone slowly leaned back.

"...Them?"

The figure who had introduced the document nodded.

"An organization operating beyond the usual arrangements."

"Are they involved?"

"We don't know."

"Then why bring them into this meeting?"

The answer came after a brief pause.

"Because their activities have begun to overlap with ours."

Silence.

The black marking remained on the table.

For several seconds, nobody touched it.

Then someone spoke.

"Have they made contact?"

"Indirectly."

"With whom?"

The answer was immediate.

"Dionysus."

A few figures exchanged glances.

One of them gave a quiet laugh.

"Then perhaps our problem has already found its solution."

Another voice interrupted.

"Or created a larger one."

Nobody disagreed.

The document was eventually collected.

The black hand disappeared beneath the other papers.

But the symbol remained in the minds of everyone present.

An organization that had existed for some time.

An organization that rarely appeared in public discussions.

An organization whose members were seldom identified.

And an organization whose leader had apparently begun taking an interest in matters that extended far beyond Orario.

The meeting continued.

Dionysus's fate was discussed.

His resources were evaluated.

His usefulness was questioned.

But the most important decision had already been made.

He would be watched.

And if he failed again, the Council would no longer concern itself with preserving his position.

It would simply find someone else.

Someone more capable.

Someone more useful.

Or someone considerably more dangerous.

### VII. The Black Hand

The headquarters of the organization was situated far from the districts where ordinary citizens conducted their daily lives.

It was not particularly impressive from the outside.

There were no guards standing prominently at the entrance, no elaborate insignia displayed above the doors, and no banners announcing the presence of an influential organization.

That was deliberate.

The Black Hand had little interest in attracting attention.

Its members operated through intermediaries, information networks, financial arrangements, and carefully cultivated relationships.

Some were merchants.

Others were adventurers.

There were even individuals who occupied respectable positions within the institutions of the city.

Their connections were extensive.

Their identities were difficult to establish.

And their objectives were rarely discussed outside their own circles.

Deep within the building, a man stood before a large table.

Several documents had been arranged across its surface.

Reports.

Maps.

Correspondence.

Names.

The information was organized with an almost unsettling degree of precision.

A subordinate stood several steps behind him.

"The Council has begun investigating the recent developments surrounding Dionysus."

The man did not immediately respond.

He was considerably larger than the subordinate.

Even from behind, his physical presence was difficult to ignore. His shoulders were broad, his frame imposing, and his posture suggested a man who had spent an extraordinary amount of time developing his body.

He wore dark clothing.

Nothing particularly elaborate.

There was something almost deliberate about the simplicity.

"Have they discovered anything useful?"

"Not yet."

"Then they are wasting their time."

His voice was deep.

Calm.

Unhurried.

The subordinate lowered his head.

"There is another matter."

The man continued examining the documents.

"Speak."

"The Filvis situation."

A brief pause.

"Two separate identities. One associated with Dionysus, the other with Bell Cranel. Their circumstances appear to have diverged considerably."

The man finally looked up.

"Bell Cranel."

"Yes."

He considered the name.

Then returned his attention to the documents.

"Interesting."

The subordinate hesitated.

"Should we intervene?"

"No."

The answer was immediate.

The man picked up a report.

"Not yet."

He examined the information.

There were several developments worth considering.

Dionysus's declining influence.

The unusual circumstances surrounding the Filvis identities.

Bell Cranel's increasing involvement in matters that had attracted the attention of several influential groups.

And the possibility that the young adventurer had begun investigating subjects that certain people would prefer to remain undiscovered.

The man placed the report down.

"People reveal considerably more about themselves when they believe they are making progress."

He moved toward another section of the table.

"Let them investigate."

The subordinate remained silent.

The man continued.

"Let Dionysus become desperate. Let the Council become impatient. Let the adventurer believe he is approaching an answer."

He paused.

"Every decision creates an opportunity."

The subordinate lowered his head.

"Understood."

He turned and left the chamber.

The heavy door closed behind him.

Silence returned.

The man remained alone.

He slowly rolled his shoulders, then placed both hands against the edge of the table.

His reflection was barely visible in the darkened glass beyond it.

There was no insignia on his clothing.

No title.

No name.

Only the faint outline of an enormous figure standing beneath the dim illumination.

He examined the documents one final time.

Then he spoke quietly.

"Purification..."

A small, almost amused breath escaped him.

"What an interesting word."

He turned away from the table.

The Black Hand had existed long before Dionysus began losing control of his affairs.

It would continue existing long after his current difficulties had been resolved.

The organization did not depend on a single god, a single Familia, or a single political arrangement.

It depended on something much simpler.

People.

Their ambitions.

Their weaknesses.

Their willingness to sacrifice others for something they considered important.

And the man who stood at its head understood those things exceptionally well.

He walked toward the exit.

The door opened.

For a brief moment, the light from the corridor illuminated his enormous silhouette.

Then he disappeared into the darkness.

Behind him, the documents remained undisturbed.

One report concerned Dionysus.

Another concerned Bell Cranel.

A third contained information about the two Filvis identities.

And beneath them all lay a final folder.

Its contents were considerably older.

Its pages concerned research that had begun long before the current situation.

On its cover was a simple designation.

**THE BLACK HAND — PROJECT: SUCCESSION**

The final page contained a handwritten note.

*The creation of an ideal successor requires the elimination of unnecessary variables.*

Beneath it was a signature.

Not a name.

Just a symbol.

A black hand.

### VIII. An Uncomfortable Discovery

Back at the Hearth Manor, Bell was still working.

The afternoon had gradually become evening.

The sunlight entering through the windows had turned orange, casting long shadows across the floor and illuminating the scattered documents.

Ein sat nearby.

She had been watching him for some time.

"You haven't stopped."

Bell glanced at her.

"I have."

"When?"

"Earlier."

"How long ago?"

He considered the question.

"...A while."

Ein narrowed her eyes.

"You're lying."

Bell laughed.

"Probably."

She approached the table and examined the documents.

There were several diagrams, notes, and observations.

Some concerned her condition.

Others concerned the relationship between magical phenomena and inherited characteristics.

She looked at the page Bell had been examining earlier.

"What is this?"

"Something I'm investigating."

"Is it important?"

"Potentially."

Ein read the heading.

Then looked at him.

"Eugenics?"

Bell nodded.

"Have you heard of it?"

"A little."

He leaned back.

"It's an idea that involves attempting to improve a population by controlling which hereditary characteristics are encouraged or discouraged."

Ein examined the document.

"That sounds rather complicated."

"It is."

"Is it necessarily bad?"

Bell considered the question.

"No. Studying hereditary characteristics isn't inherently bad. Understanding how traits are passed down can be useful."

He tapped the paper.

"But deciding that certain people are inherently more valuable than others is an entirely different matter."

Ein looked at him.

Bell continued.

"Imagine a society where children are valued according to the abilities their parents possess. Imagine families being encouraged to produce children with particular characteristics because someone believes those children would be more useful."

His expression became serious.

"What happens to the ones who don't meet those expectations?"

Ein remained silent.

"And what happens when the people making those decisions acquire enough power to determine who is allowed to have a family in the first place?"

He shook his head.

"That's not something I want to see."

Ein looked down at the document again.

Then she slowly moved closer.

"Bell."

"Hm?"

"Do you think someone would actually do that?"

Bell glanced toward the window.

"I hope not."

He paused.

"But hoping something doesn't exist isn't a particularly reliable way of investigating it."

Ein nodded.

She understood.

There was something else, however, that had caught her attention.

The documents contained references to magical compatibility and inherited characteristics.

She looked toward the notes concerning her own condition.

Then back at Bell.

"Do you think this is connected to me?"

Bell did not immediately answer.

"I don't know."

He closed the notebook.

"But I'm going to find out."

Ein watched him for a moment.

Then she reached for his hand.

Bell looked down.

Her expression was gentle.

"Then don't forget to take care of yourself, too."

He smiled.

"I'll try."

"No."

She shook her head.

"Promise."

Bell laughed softly.

"All right. I promise."

Outside, the city continued its ordinary evening routine.

People returned home.

Taverns filled with adventurers.

Merchants counted their earnings.

And somewhere beyond the familiar streets, several organizations were beginning to move.

Dionysus was becoming desperate.

The Council was losing patience.

Filvis was preparing to betray the god she had once served.

And the Black Hand had begun examining Bell Cranel.

For the moment, Bell knew none of this.

He was still concerned with a much smaller problem.

Finding a way to return Ein to normal without destroying the person he wanted to save.

He looked at the documents once more.

Then wrote a final note beneath his earlier observations.

**The objective is not to create something better. It is to ensure that the person survives the process.**

He put down his pen.

For some reason, he couldn't shake the feeling that the distinction would become increasingly important.

He simply didn't know how soon.

*End of Chapter Seventy-Two.*
