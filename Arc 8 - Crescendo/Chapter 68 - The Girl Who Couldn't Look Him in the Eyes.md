DANMACHI: RETELLING OF THE LEGEND

Chapter Sixty-Eight — The Girl Who Couldn’t Look Him in the Eyes

The first thing Lefiya noticed when she opened her eyes was that the
crystals were still glowing.

They had not dimmed.

Not that she had expected them to. The underground environment did not
seem particularly interested in following the natural rhythm of the
world above. There was no sunrise to announce the beginning of another
day, no gradual fading of darkness, and no familiar warmth creeping
through a window to coax someone out of bed.

There was only the gentle radiance of the crystals embedded throughout
the cavern walls.

Their light was pale, almost blue, and sufficiently bright to illuminate
the camp without making it feel exposed. It reflected off the uneven
stone surfaces, scattered across the ground, and caught on the
occasional movement of people preparing for the day.

Lefiya lay still for a moment.

Then she sighed.

Another morning.

Or perhaps it was afternoon.

She had stopped trusting her sense of time since entering this place.

The expedition had already distorted her usual routine. Sleep came when
exhaustion demanded it, meals followed when circumstances permitted, and
the distinction between one day and another had gradually become less
meaningful than the tasks waiting ahead.

It was an unpleasant adjustment for someone who preferred a reasonably
organized schedule.

She pushed herself upright, brushed a few strands of hair away from her
face, and looked toward the distant formations of crystal.

The cavern was peaceful.

Strangely peaceful.

That was what made it unsettling.

There was something unnatural about a place so deep underground
possessing an atmosphere almost reminiscent of a quiet forest clearing.
The crystals provided enough light to soften the darkness, while the
distant sounds of movement gave the camp a sense of ordinary life.

For a moment, she could almost forget where they were.

Almost.

Her thoughts eventually drifted toward yesterday.

More specifically, toward a certain white-haired adventurer.

Lefiya pressed her lips together.

Bell Cranel.

Even thinking his name seemed to bring a particular collection of
memories to the surface.

His unusual magic. His composure. The way he approached difficult
situations without appearing particularly impressed by his own
accomplishments. The peculiar combination of preparation and
improvisation that made his actions difficult to predict.

He was not simply powerful.

That was something she had understood for quite some time.

Power could be measured, compared, and eventually surpassed. A
sufficiently talented mage could develop greater output, improve
efficiency, or discover a more effective application of an existing
spell.

But Bell possessed something that was considerably harder to quantify.

He looked at problems differently.

Sometimes, Lefiya suspected, he looked at them from angles that other
people had not even considered.

And that was precisely what fascinated her.

She remembered the first time she had encountered him, long before the
present expedition. The impression had remained with her through the
passing years, gradually changing as the boy she remembered became an
increasingly accomplished adventurer.

She had wondered what sort of person he would become.

Now she knew.

Or, at least, she knew enough to understand that she still had much to
learn.

Lefiya lowered her gaze toward her hands.

There was something rather frustrating about admiring someone whose
accomplishments seemed to create more questions than answers.

She wanted to understand how he thought.

How he prepared.

How he decided which approach to take when confronted with an unfamiliar
opponent.

And, perhaps most importantly, how he managed to remain so composed
while doing things that would leave most adventurers struggling to catch
their breath.

She smiled faintly.

It was admirable.

Genuinely admirable.

Then another thought occurred to her.

She might encounter him today.

Her smile disappeared.

“…”

Lefiya slowly pulled her blanket closer.

That was unfortunate.

Not because she disliked speaking with him.

Quite the opposite.

She wanted to speak with him.

The problem was that wanting to do something and actually managing to do
it were two entirely different matters.

Whenever Bell was nearby, her thoughts became unnecessarily complicated.
She began considering things that would ordinarily never occupy her
attention: whether she was standing properly, whether her expression
looked strange, whether her words sounded intelligent, and whether she
had somehow managed to embarrass herself before even opening her mouth.

It was ridiculous.

She knew it was ridiculous.

Unfortunately, knowing that did very little to improve the situation.

Lefiya finally stood and began preparing for the day.

There was no reason to become nervous over an ordinary conversation.

After all, she was Lefiya Viridis.

She was a mage.

An accomplished adventurer.

She had faced monsters, endured dangerous expeditions, and participated
in battles where hesitation could have cost lives.

Surely she could manage a simple conversation with one person.

One very impressive person.

One person whose opinion she cared about considerably more than she
wanted to admit.

She stopped adjusting her clothing.

“…This is going to be difficult.”

The camp had already begun to stir by the time Lefiya finished
preparing.

The underground cavern carried its usual mixture of sounds: footsteps
against stone, the occasional clatter of equipment, quiet conversations,
and the distant movement of people attending to their responsibilities.

She stepped away from her resting area and allowed herself a moment to
observe the surroundings.

The expedition had changed the atmosphere of the group.

There was a certain familiarity now, a gradual acceptance of the strange
environment and the routines it demanded. People were learning how to
operate within the limitations of their surroundings, and even the most
unusual circumstances were beginning to acquire an ordinary rhythm.

Yet yesterday’s events had left their mark.

Bell and Ais.

Their sparring match.

Lefiya had not been in a position to witness every detail of the
confrontation herself, and she refused to pretend otherwise. What she
knew had come through the information available afterward and the
accounts of those involved.

Still, even the broad outline was enough to occupy her thoughts.

Bell had fought Ais.

Not merely exchanged a few blows or demonstrated a technique.

He had challenged her swordsmanship, recognized the weaknesses in her
execution of the Waldstein technique, and demonstrated a more refined
application.

Then there had been Ariel.

Ais’s wind had transformed the nature of the confrontation, increasing
her speed and changing the pressure of every exchange.

Bell, however, had not simply attempted to overcome her with greater
force.

He had controlled his own wind and channeled it through Crimson Order.

And then there was the black wind.

That detail had particularly caught Lefiya’s attention.

Bell had used Dispel to stop it.

She understood the general significance of magical interference and the
importance of disrupting an opponent’s spell, but the exact
circumstances of that exchange were something she wanted to understand
more clearly.

There was also the weapon itself.

Crimson Order.

A rapier made for Goddess Astrea.

The circumstances surrounding its creation were not something Lefiya
knew in complete detail, but the purpose behind the gift was meaningful
enough to leave an impression.

Bell had made something for someone who had suffered a terrible loss.

And she had declined it.

Lefiya wondered what that conversation had been like.

Perhaps the goddess had considered the weapon too valuable.

Perhaps she had believed there was another reason to refuse.

Whatever the explanation, the weapon had remained with Bell.

She looked toward the other side of the camp.

The sparring match had apparently meant something to Ais as well.

That was another matter she wanted to understand.

Not because she doubted Ais’s strength.

She had never doubted it.

But Ais was not someone who easily expressed her thoughts. When
something affected her deeply, the change was often subtle enough that
people unfamiliar with her might overlook it entirely.

Lefiya did not want to make that mistake.

She decided she would speak with her when the opportunity arose.

For now, there was something else she needed to settle.

She needed to stop thinking about Bell.

At least until she had finished breakfast.

That seemed reasonable.

It lasted approximately thirty seconds.

Ais was easier to approach than Lefiya expected.

Not because the swordswoman was particularly approachable, but because
she rarely made ordinary conversations more complicated than necessary.

Lefiya found her a little later, sitting quietly near the edge of the
camp.

Ais appeared to be examining her sword.

There were no obvious signs of injury, and her movements seemed as
composed as ever. If anything, she looked unusually thoughtful.

Lefiya approached.

“Miss Ais?”

Ais raised her head.

“Lefiya.”

“Good morning.”

“Good morning.”

Lefiya settled beside her, leaving enough room not to intrude.

For a little while, neither spoke.

It was not an uncomfortable silence. Ais had never been particularly
troubled by quiet moments, and Lefiya had grown accustomed to them.

Her eyes eventually drifted toward the sword.

“Are you all right?”

Ais nodded.

“Yes.”

“That’s good.”

Lefiya hesitated.

She had several questions, but she wanted to choose her words carefully.
Asking whether Ais was disappointed would have been presumptuous. Asking
whether she had learned anything would have sounded like she was
treating the swordswoman’s defeat as some sort of examination.

Neither seemed appropriate.

Instead, she settled on something simpler.

“About yesterday…”

Ais looked at her.

“The sparring match?”

Lefiya nodded.

“Yes. I heard about it.”

Ais lowered her eyes toward the sword.

“It was difficult.”

Lefiya waited.

Ais continued after a moment.

“Bell understood my technique.”

There was no resentment in her voice.

Only a quiet acknowledgment.

Lefiya tilted her head.

“His understanding of the Waldstein technique surprised you?”

Ais nodded.

“He saw what I was doing wrong.”

She paused.

“Then he showed me.”

Lefiya considered that.

It was one thing to recognize an opponent’s weakness during combat. It
was another to understand the underlying principles well enough to
demonstrate a better application.

That required more than quick reflexes.

It required knowledge.

“Did you find his explanation useful?”

“Yes.”

Ais looked at her sword again.

“I want to try it.”

Lefiya smiled.

“Then I suppose the match was worthwhile.”

Ais nodded.

“Yes.”

There was another pause.

Lefiya watched her companion’s expression.

Ais did not look particularly troubled by losing. That did not mean the
defeat had been insignificant, but she seemed more interested in what
she could take from the experience than in the outcome itself.

That was reassuring.

“Did his wind give you trouble?” Lefiya asked.

Ais looked up.

“Yes.”

“Because he channeled it through the rapier?”

Ais nodded.

“It changed the distance.”

Lefiya’s eyebrows rose.

“The distance?”

Ais made a small movement with her hand, as though demonstrating the
direction of a strike.

“His wind reached farther.”

She paused.

“And pushed me back.”

Lefiya considered the explanation.

That was an interesting application.

Rather than treating wind as something that simply increased movement or
surrounded the body, Bell had used it to influence the effective reach
and force of his weapon.

A weapon did not necessarily need to make contact to influence an
opponent’s movement.

The space around it could become part of the attack.

It was a simple observation, perhaps, but its implications were
considerable.

“That’s clever,” Lefiya murmured.

Ais nodded.

“Yes.”

“And the black wind?”

Ais looked down.

“Bell stopped it.”

“With Dispel?”

“Yes.”

Lefiya frowned slightly.

She wanted to ask more, but Ais’s explanation was limited by the fact
that she had not yet described the phenomenon itself.

Perhaps that was something Bell could explain.

Or perhaps she would have to observe it herself if another opportunity
arose.

“Did you learn anything else?”

Ais thought about it.

“Yes.”

She looked toward the cavern ceiling.

“He’s strong.”

Lefiya smiled faintly.

“That much is rather obvious.”

Ais turned toward her.

“But not only strong.”

Lefiya’s smile softened.

Ais continued.

“He understands.”

“Understands?”

“The sword. Magic. People.”

She paused.

“How they fight.”

Lefiya looked at her companion.

There was something unusual about the way Ais described him. She was not
particularly expressive, and her words were often direct, but that did
not make them insignificant.

Ais had recognized something important.

Perhaps she had recognized the same quality that had fascinated Lefiya
for years.

Bell did not merely react to the things happening around him.

He tried to understand them.

And once he understood something, he looked for ways to use that
knowledge.

Lefiya lowered her eyes.

“I see.”

Ais looked at her.

“You’re interested in him.”

Lefiya stiffened.

“I—”

The swordswoman waited.

Lefiya took a breath.

“His magic and fighting style are certainly interesting.”

Ais nodded.

“Yes.”

There was no teasing in her expression.

No hidden implication.

She had simply made an observation.

Lefiya relaxed slightly.

“I would like to understand how he approaches unfamiliar techniques. He
seems capable of identifying things that other people overlook.”

Ais considered that.

“Ask him.”

Lefiya’s shoulders became rigid.

“…I suppose I could.”

Ais tilted her head.

“Why not?”

Lefiya looked at her.

The answer was obvious.

Because asking Bell a question required actually speaking to Bell.

And speaking to Bell required surviving the experience of being close
enough to hear his answer.

Ais waited.

Lefiya looked away.

“It’s not that simple.”

“Why?”

“Because…”

She searched for an explanation that would not make her sound completely
unreasonable.

“…I haven’t decided how to ask.”

Ais blinked.

Then she nodded.

“Okay.”

That was all.

No further interrogation.

No attempt to understand the complicated chain of thoughts that had led
Lefiya to such a conclusion.

For that, Lefiya was grateful.

She looked toward Ais again.

“By the way, you don’t seem particularly upset.”

Ais considered the question.

“I’m not.”

“Even though you lost?”

Ais shook her head.

“I wanted to win.”

She looked down at her sword.

“But I learned something.”

Lefiya smiled.

“Then you haven’t lost everything.”

Ais nodded.

“Yes.”

For a moment, they sat quietly.

Then Ais spoke again.

“Bell is my hero.”

Lefiya turned toward her.

The words were delivered simply, without hesitation or embarrassment.

Ais did not elaborate.

She did not need to.

Lefiya studied her face, searching for the meaning behind the statement
without attempting to impose one of her own.

A hero.

That was a rather significant word.

She remembered the many stories associated with the title. People who
had overcome impossible odds, protected others, or accomplished things
that seemed beyond the reach of ordinary adventurers.

But Ais had not said Bell was powerful.

She had not called him the strongest.

She had called him her hero.

Lefiya lowered her eyes.

There was something deeply personal about that distinction.

She respected Ais too much to pry into a sentiment that had clearly come
from somewhere important.

Instead, she smiled.

“Then I hope he continues to live up to that.”

Ais nodded.

“Me too.”

The conversation ended naturally after that.

Lefiya eventually stood, leaving Ais to her thoughts.

As she walked away, she found herself considering the swordswoman’s
words.

Bell was her hero.

Lefiya did not know precisely what that meant to Ais.

But she understood that the statement mattered.

And perhaps that was enough.

The problem with Bell Cranel was that he could be perfectly ordinary.

That was the conclusion Lefiya reached approximately an hour later.

It was an absurd conclusion.

She knew that.

But she was beginning to suspect that his ordinary behavior was somehow
more difficult to deal with than his extraordinary accomplishments.

The encounter happened near one of the camp’s storage areas.

Lefiya had been occupied with a small personal task when she noticed
someone approaching from the opposite direction.

White hair.

A familiar silhouette.

A face she had spent considerably more time thinking about than she
would ever willingly admit.

Bell.

He was carrying something in one hand.

Lefiya recognized him immediately.

And then, rather inconveniently, he noticed her.

“Morning, Lefiya.”

Her thoughts stopped.

Not gradually.

Not politely.

They simply ceased functioning.

“Ah—!”

She straightened.

“Good morning, Bell!”

The response came out considerably louder than intended.

Bell blinked.

Then smiled.

“You’re up early.”

Lefiya looked at him.

At least, she attempted to.

“Yes. I mean, not particularly early. I was already awake, so…”

She stopped.

Why had she said that?

It was a perfectly ordinary greeting.

He had not asked for a detailed explanation of her sleeping schedule.

“Ah, I see.”

Bell nodded.

He did not seem particularly concerned.

That was good.

That was very good.

Unfortunately, it left Lefiya with the responsibility of continuing the
conversation.

She became aware of several things simultaneously.

Her posture.

Her hands.

The position of her feet.

The fact that she had been staring at him for slightly too long.

And, most importantly, the possibility that her expression looked
strange.

She immediately looked toward the object Bell was carrying.

It was safer.

“What are you carrying?”

Bell glanced down.

“This?”

He lifted it slightly.

“Just something I was moving.”

“Oh.”

A pause.

“Is it heavy?”

Bell looked at the object.

Then back at her.

“Not really.”

“Ah.”

Another pause.

Lefiya wanted to disappear.

Not permanently.

Just temporarily.

Perhaps for five minutes.

Enough time to reorganize her thoughts and return as a functioning
person.

Bell, apparently unaware of the internal disaster taking place before
him, shifted the object in his hand.

“Have you eaten yet?”

Lefiya blinked.

“…Pardon?”

“Breakfast.”

“Oh! Yes. I mean, not yet.”

Bell nodded.

“Then you should probably eat something.”

“Yes.”

She nodded.

“Of course.”

He smiled.

“Don’t forget.”

Lefiya watched him continue walking.

The conversation was over.

That was all.

A greeting.

A question.

A suggestion to eat.

Nothing particularly remarkable had happened.

And yet she remained standing in the same place for several seconds.

Then she closed her eyes.

What was that?

She pressed her fingers against her forehead.

He asked whether I had eaten, and I responded as though he had presented
me with a difficult magical theorem. Why did I say yes and then
immediately say not yet? That was not even an answer. It was two
contradictory answers placed next to each other and delivered with
complete confidence.

She exhaled.

And why did I look at the object instead of his face? He was speaking to
me. Looking at someone while they speak is perfectly ordinary. It is not
an advanced social skill. It is not something that requires years of
specialized training.

Her cheeks warmed.

I really hope he didn’t notice.

Then she remembered his final words.

Don’t forget.

Lefiya stared at the ground.

“…He was being considerate.”

That realization somehow made the situation worse.

Not because there was anything wrong with it.

But because she appreciated the gesture.

And because she wanted him to know that she appreciated it.

Which meant she would probably spend the next several minutes thinking
about whether she should have said thank you.

She covered her face.

This was becoming exhausting.

Lefiya eventually managed to eat.

That was an achievement in itself.

She had spent an unreasonable amount of time replaying the encounter,
examining every word and gesture as though she were reviewing the
casting sequence of a particularly complicated spell.

Eventually, hunger had won.

It was difficult to maintain a dignified internal crisis when one’s
stomach was demanding attention.

After finishing her meal, she decided to find somewhere quiet.

There were several things she wanted to think about, and Bell’s earlier
encounter had reminded her of an important one.

She needed to ask him something.

Not merely greet him.

Not simply exchange a few words.

She wanted to have an actual conversation.

A conversation about magic.

That was something she understood.

It was something she could approach without feeling completely out of
her depth.

At least, theoretically.

She found a relatively quiet section of the cavern where the crystal
formations created a soft pattern of light across the stone. The air was
cool, and the distant sounds of the camp were muted by the surrounding
formations.

Lefiya settled down and began reviewing what she knew about the previous
day’s confrontation.

Crimson Order.

Ariel.

The black wind.

Dispel.

There was a considerable difference between understanding the names of
abilities and understanding their practical applications.

As a mage, Lefiya was familiar with the importance of knowing what an
opponent could do. Magical output, chant duration, effective range,
elemental properties, and the circumstances under which a spell could be
interrupted were all relevant considerations.

But Bell’s approach seemed to involve more than simply identifying an
opponent’s strengths and weaknesses.

He appeared to consider the relationship between different abilities.

How one technique could alter the effectiveness of another.

How a weapon could become an extension of magical output.

How a seemingly defensive action might create an offensive opportunity.
It was a way of thinking that interested her.

And it raised a question.

When Bell channeled wind through Crimson Order, was he primarily
increasing the weapon’s reach, influencing the direction of the strike,
or using the rapier as a medium to control the movement of the
surrounding air?

Perhaps all three.

But she could not be certain.

She frowned.

There was also the matter of Dispel.

Interrupting an opponent’s magic was not necessarily difficult in
principle. The difficulty came from identifying the appropriate moment
and applying the countermeasure without exposing oneself to another
attack.

Ais had already been moving.

The black wind had appeared.

Bell had reacted.

That sequence suggested a considerable degree of observation and timing.

Lefiya rested her chin against her hand.

There was a reason she wanted to ask him.

Not because she expected him to reveal some extraordinary secret.

She wanted to understand the reasoning behind his decisions.

That was where the real value lay.

A spell could be learned.

A technique could be practiced.

But learning how another person approached a problem was considerably
more difficult.

And perhaps that was why Bell fascinated her.

He did not seem to approach combat as a collection of predetermined
answers.

He adapted.

He observed.

He experimented.

Then he made decisions.

Lefiya smiled slightly.

That was something worth learning.

“You’re thinking rather hard.”

Lefiya nearly jumped.

She turned.

Bell stood several steps away.

For one horrifying instant, she wondered whether he had somehow heard
her thoughts.

Then she remembered that he could not possibly have done so.

“Bell!”

He looked at her.

“Sorry. Did I interrupt?”

“No!”

The answer came too quickly.

Lefiya straightened.

“I mean, no. You didn’t interrupt anything important.”

Bell glanced at the empty space beside her.

“Can I sit?”

“…Of course.”

He settled nearby.

Lefiya immediately became aware of the distance between them.

It was perfectly reasonable.

There was enough space for both of them to sit comfortably.

There was absolutely nothing unusual about it.

And yet her mind had decided that this was now an important situation
requiring careful management.

She looked at the crystal formations.

Then at the ground.

Then, finally, at Bell.

He seemed relaxed.

That helped.

A little.

“You were thinking about something?” he asked.

Lefiya nodded.

“Yes.”

“Something about the expedition?”

“Not exactly.”

She hesitated.

Then she took a breath.

“Actually, I wanted to ask you about yesterday’s sparring match.”

Bell looked at her.

“With Ais?”

“Yes.”

He nodded.

“All right.”

The simplicity of his response was almost unfair.

Lefiya had spent a considerable amount of time preparing herself to ask
that question, and he had accepted it as though she had merely asked for
directions.

She folded her hands over her knees.

“I wanted to understand something about your approach to combat.”

Bell tilted his head slightly.

“My approach?”

“Yes. Particularly the way you used Crimson Order.”

His expression changed just a little.

Not dramatically.

Rather, he seemed to be considering which part of the subject she was
interested in.

Lefiya continued.

“When you channeled wind through the rapier, were you attempting to
extend the weapon’s effective reach, or were you primarily controlling
the direction and force of the air around it?”

Bell looked at her for a moment.

Then he smiled.

“That’s a good question.”

Lefiya’s heart gave an unpleasant little jump.

He said it was a good question.

She resisted the temptation to look away.

“Thank you.”

Bell rested his hand against his knee.

“It wasn’t really one thing.”

Lefiya listened carefully.

“The weapon was useful because it gave me something to work with. I
could direct the wind along the line of the blade, but that didn’t mean
I was simply making the rapier longer.”

“So the additional reach was a consequence rather than the primary
objective?”

“Something like that.”

He considered his words.

“The important thing was controlling where the force went. If I pushed
too much wind in the wrong direction, I could make the attack stronger
without making it more effective.”

Lefiya’s eyes brightened.

That made sense.

A powerful attack was not automatically an accurate one.

An increase in output did not guarantee an improvement in practical
effectiveness.

In fact, excessive force could make an otherwise controlled technique
more difficult to manage.

She nodded.

“So you were adjusting the distribution of force rather than simply
increasing its magnitude.”

Bell looked at her.

“Yes. That’s a better way to put it.”

Lefiya felt a small measure of satisfaction.

Good.

She could still hold a conversation.

She could still think.

Her mind had not completely abandoned her.

“Then may I ask something else?”

“Sure.”

“How much of that control came from your familiarity with wind magic,
and how much came from experimenting with the weapon itself?”

Bell looked down briefly.

“Both, probably.”

He paused.

“I didn’t know exactly how it would behave when I first tried it.”

Lefiya leaned forward slightly.

“You experimented?”

“Yes.”

“Before using it against Ais?”

Bell nodded.

“Of course.”

There was no arrogance in his answer.

It was simply a practical statement.

Lefiya found herself smiling.

That was precisely the sort of answer she had wanted.

“Then you didn’t develop the application during the fight.”

“Not entirely.”

He glanced toward the rapier.

“I had an idea of what I wanted to do. But knowing how something should
work and knowing how it actually behaves are different things.”

Lefiya nodded slowly.

She understood that distinction very well.

Mages often developed theories about how a spell should behave under
certain conditions. Yet practical experimentation frequently revealed
complications that theoretical understanding alone could not account
for.

A spell might have an unexpected interaction with the environment.

Its output might fluctuate.

Its effective range might differ from what the caster anticipated.

And the difference between a successful experiment and a failed one
could be as simple as an overlooked variable.

Bell seemed to approach his equipment in much the same way.

“You test your assumptions.”

He looked at her.

“When I can.”

“And if something doesn’t work?”

“I try to understand why.”

Lefiya smiled.

“That’s a very mage-like answer.”

Bell laughed quietly.

“Is it?”

“Certainly. A proper understanding of magic requires more than
memorizing incantations or repeating established techniques. One must
understand the principles behind them.”

She paused.

Then added, with a little more confidence:

“Although I suppose your methods are somewhat unconventional.”

Bell looked amused.

“I’ve heard that before.”

“I imagine you have.”

For the first time since the conversation began, Lefiya felt herself
settling into it.

The subject was familiar.

She was no longer simply reacting to Bell’s presence.

She was discussing something she genuinely understood.

And that distinction mattered.

She continued.

“There’s something else I wanted to ask. About Dispel.”

Bell nodded.

“What about it?”

“Your timing.”

She looked at him more directly.

“When the black wind appeared, you stopped it before it could continue
affecting the fight. Was that because you recognized the nature of the
magic, or because you identified the moment when disrupting it would be
most effective?”

Bell was quiet for a moment.

“I recognized enough to know that letting it continue would be
dangerous.”

Lefiya listened.

“But I also had to consider the timing. Disrupting something too early
or too late can make a difference.”

“So you were judging the immediate threat rather than attempting to
understand the entire phenomenon first.”

“Yes.”

He shrugged slightly.

“You don’t always have time to understand everything before making a
decision.”

Lefiya considered that.

It was an important point.

In a controlled environment, a mage could spend hours examining an
unfamiliar spell. They could study its properties, test its limits, and
develop an appropriate countermeasure.

Combat did not offer that luxury.

Sometimes, the most important decision was not the one that produced the
greatest understanding.

It was the one that prevented the situation from becoming worse.

“That sounds rather difficult.”

“It can be.”

Bell looked toward the cavern.

“Especially when you don’t know what the other person is trying to do.”

Lefiya nodded.

“Then observation becomes more important.”

“Definitely.”

He looked back at her.

“Although I think you already understand that.”

Lefiya blinked.

“Me?”

“You’re a mage. You have to pay attention to more than the spell
itself.”

She stared at him.

Bell continued without seeming to notice the effect his words had
produced.

“You need to understand what the person casting it is trying to
accomplish. The spell is only part of the problem.”

Lefiya’s expression softened.

That was true.

A mage’s spell could reveal something about their intentions, but it did
not necessarily reveal everything.

The caster’s positioning, their movement, their timing, and even the
direction of their attention could provide information.

A spell was not an isolated phenomenon.

It was an action performed by someone.

And understanding the person behind it could be just as important as
understanding the magic.

She smiled.

“That’s a rather interesting way of looking at it.”

Bell shrugged.

“I suppose.”

Lefiya looked down at her hands.

She had wanted to ask him about his abilities.

Instead, she was discovering something about his way of thinking.

It was more valuable than she had anticipated.

“May I ask one final question?”

“Go ahead.”

She hesitated briefly.

Then looked toward him.

“Why do you bother understanding other people’s techniques so
thoroughly?”

Bell blinked.

Lefiya continued.

“I understand the practical advantages. Recognizing an opponent’s
weaknesses, adapting to their fighting style, and identifying
opportunities are all important. But you seem interested in
understanding the techniques themselves, even when doing so isn’t
immediately necessary.”

Bell did not answer at once.

His gaze drifted toward the distant crystals.

“I don’t think it’s a waste of time.”

Lefiya waited.

“Every person has a reason for fighting the way they do. Sometimes it’s
because of their training. Sometimes it’s because of their abilities.
Sometimes it’s because they’ve found something that works for them.”

He paused.

“If I understand that, I have a better idea of what they might do next.”

Lefiya nodded.

That was reasonable.

But Bell continued.

“And sometimes I can learn something from them.”

She looked at him.

“Even if they’re weaker than you?”

Bell turned toward her.

“Of course.”

The answer came without hesitation.

“Why wouldn’t I?”

Lefiya found herself momentarily speechless.

Bell smiled faintly.

“Someone doesn’t have to be stronger than me to know something I don’t.”

The words were simple.

Yet they settled into her thoughts with surprising weight.

There was no need for Bell to make himself appear superior.

He did not treat another person’s experience as insignificant simply
because their abilities were different.

He was willing to learn.

From opponents.

From allies.

From anyone who possessed something worth understanding.

Lefiya looked at him for several seconds.

Then she smiled.

“…I think that’s a very good way to approach things.”

Bell nodded.

“Thank you.”

A comfortable silence followed.

Not the strained silence of their earlier encounter.

Not the sort that required Lefiya to search desperately for something to
say.

Just silence.

She allowed herself to enjoy it.

Eventually, Bell stood.

“I should get back.”

Lefiya nodded.

“Of course.”

“Thanks for the conversation.”

Her eyes widened slightly.

“Ah?”

Bell smiled.

“It was interesting.”

Lefiya stared at him.

Then managed a small smile of her own.

“I’m glad.”

“See you later.”

“Yes. See you.”

Bell walked away.

This time, Lefiya did not immediately cover her face.

She simply watched him leave.

Only after he disappeared from view did she release the breath she had
apparently been holding.

“…That went well.”

She looked at the empty space beside her.

Then smiled.

It really had.

The conversation remained with Lefiya longer than she expected.

She had initially intended to spend some time thinking about Bell’s
fighting style. Instead, she found herself considering his final answer.

Someone doesn’t have to be stronger than me to know something I don’t.

It was a simple principle.

Yet it was one that deserved more consideration than she had initially
given it.

Lefiya had spent years studying magic, refining her abilities, and
developing her understanding of combat. She had trained under
accomplished individuals and learned from experiences that had
challenged her assumptions.

She was not inexperienced.

She was not weak.

And she certainly had no reason to dismiss her own accomplishments.

Still, standing beside people like Bell and Ais sometimes made her feel
as though she had barely begun.

Ais possessed a remarkable understanding of swordsmanship. Her instincts
in battle were extraordinary, and her ability to move through combat
with such precision was something Lefiya could only admire.

Bell was different.

His approach was less predictable, his methods often unconventional, and
his understanding of different disciplines seemed to extend beyond the
boundaries most adventurers accepted.

They were both impressive.

But they were not impressive in the same way.

And that was something Lefiya needed to remember.

She looked down at her hands.

There was a tendency among adventurers to compare themselves with
others. It was natural, perhaps unavoidable. People wanted to know where
they stood, how much they had improved, and what remained between them
and their ambitions.

But comparison could become a rather unpleasant habit.

If she measured herself exclusively against the people she admired, she
would always find something she lacked.

There would always be someone faster.

Someone stronger.

Someone more experienced.

Someone who understood a particular subject better than she did.

That did not mean her own achievements were meaningless.

Lefiya had worked hard to reach her present position. Her knowledge of
magic was not something she had acquired through luck, nor had her
accomplishments been handed to her simply because she possessed talent.

Every improvement had required effort.

Every successful battle had taught her something.

Every failure had given her another reason to continue.

She had earned her place.

And she wanted to earn an even better one.

Perhaps that was the distinction she had been missing.

She did not need to become Bell.

She did not need to fight like Ais.

She wanted to become someone who could stand beside them without feeling
that her presence was somehow inadequate.

Someone whose abilities could make a difference.

Someone whose judgment could be trusted.

Someone who could contribute rather than merely admire what others
accomplished.

Lefiya raised her head.

The crystals above her continued to glow.

Their light was unchanged, indifferent to the passage of time.

Yet something inside her had settled.

She still wanted to learn from Bell.

She still wanted to understand how he approached magic and combat.

And she still wanted him to think well of her.

That last part was somewhat embarrassing.

But perhaps it was not something she needed to eliminate entirely.

Caring about another person’s opinion was not inherently wrong.

The problem was allowing that concern to prevent her from doing anything
worthwhile.

She smiled.

“Then I suppose I should work harder.”

It was a small declaration.

No grand promise.

No dramatic resolution.

Just a decision.

And for the moment, that was enough.

The second encounter happened later.

Lefiya had been making her way through the camp when she noticed Bell
standing near one of the crystal formations.

He appeared to be examining something.

She slowed.

Her first instinct was to approach.

Her second was to reconsider.

Her third was to wonder whether approaching him again so soon would make
her appear strange.

She stopped.

Then frowned at herself.

No.

She had already spoken with him earlier.

There was nothing unusual about speaking with the same person twice in
one day.

They were both members of the expedition.

They were both occupying the same camp.

There was no reason to treat an ordinary interaction as though it
required special permission.

Lefiya took a breath.

Then approached.

“Bell?”

He turned.

“Oh, Lefiya.”

He smiled.

“Something wrong?”

“No. Not at all.”

She stopped beside him.

“What are you looking at?”

“These crystals.”

He gestured toward the formation.

“I was wondering about the light.”

Lefiya looked at it.

The crystals emitted their familiar pale radiance, illuminating the
surrounding stone without any obvious source of heat.

“Are you trying to understand how they work?”

“More or less.”

Bell examined the formation again.

“I don’t know enough about them to make any conclusions.”

Lefiya nodded.

“Then I suppose you’ll need to investigate them more carefully.”

“Probably.”

He glanced toward her.

“Actually, I remembered something from our earlier conversation.”

Lefiya blinked.

“Something?”

“About understanding the principles behind magic.”

She nodded slowly.

“Yes?”

“I think you explained it better than I did.”

Lefiya stared at him.

For a moment, she could not determine whether she had heard correctly.

Then her expression softened.

“Oh.”

Bell continued.

“You made a good point. Knowing how something works is important, but
understanding why it works gives you more options.”

Lefiya felt a quiet warmth spread through her chest.

He remembered.

Not merely the fact that they had spoken, but something she had said.

It was a small detail.

An ordinary detail.

Yet it meant something to her.

“Well,” she began, then stopped herself.

There was no need to become nervous.

She knew the subject.

She understood what she wanted to say.

And she could answer him.

“That’s because the principles are what allow us to adapt. If we only
memorize the result, we’re limited to reproducing it under familiar
circumstances.”

Bell nodded.

“Exactly.”

Lefiya smiled.

“Although I suppose that applies to more than magic.”

“It does.”

He looked toward the crystal formation again.

“Probably applies to adventuring, too.”

Lefiya nodded.

“Certainly.”

A brief silence followed.

Then Bell turned toward her.

“By the way, thanks for talking with me earlier.”

She blinked.

“For what?”

“The questions.”

He smiled.

“They made me think about things I hadn’t really considered.”

Lefiya’s expression changed.

She had expected him to thank her for something practical.

Perhaps for providing information or helping with a task.

She had not expected him to say that.

“Oh.”

She lowered her gaze briefly.

Then, after a moment, looked back at him.

“You’re welcome.”

Her voice was steady.

No stuttering.

No hurried explanation.

No desperate attempt to find another sentence.

Just two words.

Bell smiled.

“See you around.”

“See you.”

He returned his attention to the crystals.

Lefiya continued on her way.

She had managed to walk several steps before realizing something.

She had looked him in the eyes.

Not for an especially long time.

Not without difficulty.

But she had done it.

And she had answered him without stumbling.

A small smile appeared on her face.

“…I did it.”

She immediately looked around.

Fortunately, nobody appeared to have heard her.

She continued walking, her steps a little lighter than before.

The underground cavern remained unchanged.

Its crystals continued illuminating the camp, and the distant sounds of
the expedition carried through the surrounding stone.

Time was still difficult to measure.

The environment remained unfamiliar.

And there were still countless things Lefiya did not understand.

But she no longer felt quite as frustrated by that fact.

There would always be something to learn.

Something to improve.

Someone whose abilities could teach her a new way of thinking.

She thought of Ais.

The swordswoman who could turn a battlefield into something resembling a
dance, each movement precise and purposeful.

Then she thought of Bell.
The adventurer who could examine an unfamiliar problem, experiment with
its possibilities, and discover an answer that others might never have
considered.

She admired them both.

For different reasons.

And she hoped to become someone who could stand beside them.

Not because she wanted to imitate their accomplishments.

But because she wanted to create achievements of her own.

Lefiya slowed near the edge of the camp.

Beyond the familiar formations, the cavern stretched into darkness, its
distant passages illuminated by scattered crystals.

For a moment, she imagined what awaited them beyond those passages.

More challenges.

More dangers.

More opportunities to discover how little they truly understood.

Perhaps Bell would encounter another unfamiliar technique.

Perhaps Ais would discover another weakness in her swordsmanship.

Perhaps Lefiya herself would face something that challenged everything
she believed about magic.

She smiled.

That was all right.

She did not need to have every answer today.

She only needed to continue learning.

Her thoughts eventually returned to Bell.

Unfortunately, some things had not changed.

She still found it difficult to maintain eye contact with him.

She still overthought ordinary conversations.

And she still suspected that the next time he greeted her unexpectedly,
her carefully arranged thoughts would scatter in every direction.

That was probably inevitable.

But she had managed to speak with him.

Properly.

She had asked questions, discussed magic, and received answers that gave
her something meaningful to consider.

More importantly, she had discovered that he was willing to learn from
her, too.

The thought made her smile.

Perhaps she had been looking at the distance between them incorrectly.

She had always wondered how far she needed to climb before she could
stand beside people like Bell and Ais.

But perhaps the answer was not to spend every moment measuring that
distance.

Perhaps it was to take another step whenever the opportunity presented
itself.

One step.

Then another.

And another.

Until one day, she might look back and realize how far she had traveled.

Lefiya turned toward the camp.

The crystal light followed her movements, casting a faint reflection
across the stone beneath her feet.

She was still the same Lefiya Viridis.

A mage who loved magic.

An adventurer who wanted to become stronger.

A girl who admired an unusually capable young man and somehow became
less articulate whenever he was within speaking distance.

That last part would probably take some time to fix.

She sighed.

Then smiled.

“Well… I’ll try again tomorrow.”

And with that, she continued walking.

She still couldn’t quite look Bell Cranel in the eyes.

Not consistently, anyway.

But for the first time, she had begun to understand that standing beside
someone she admired did not require her to stop being herself.

It only required her to keep moving forward.

And perhaps, one day, she would no longer have to look away.
